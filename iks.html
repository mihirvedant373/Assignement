📘 Chapter 1 – Short Answer Questions (4 marks)

✏️ Write crisp, point-wise for 4 marks | Each answer ~60-80 words
1. Define IKS and its core objective? 4 marks

IKS: Indian Knowledge Systems are the indigenous, systematic knowledge traditions of India developed over millennia – covering philosophy, science, medicine, arts, and spirituality. Core objective: holistic well-being (physical, mental, social, spiritual) and self-realization (Moksha). It integrates material and spiritual knowledge for harmony with nature and society.

2. What is Anubhava (experiential knowledge)?

Anubhava means direct personal experience, not just reading or hearing. Example: tasting a mango gives Anubhava, while hearing about sweetness is indirect. In IKS, higher knowledge (self-realization) requires Anubhava – transforming theory into lived wisdom. It is the highest validation of truth.

3. Explain Dharma in IKS context.

Dharma means righteous duty, moral law, and the inherent order that sustains society and cosmos. It is contextual – varies by age, occupation, and situation. Universal duties include truth and non-violence; specific duties relate to one's profession (e.g., a teacher's dharma to teach). Dharma is the foundation of a just society.

4. What is Darsana?

Darsana means "view" or "way of seeing reality". In Indian philosophy, it refers to the six orthodox (Astika) schools – Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vedanta. Each Darsana provides a distinct perspective on reality, self, liberation, and valid knowledge, all aiming for Moksha.

5. Two Astika schools?

Samkhya: Dualism of Purusha (consciousness) and Prakriti (matter); liberation through discriminative knowledge. Yoga: Practical path of eight limbs (ashtanga) – yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, etc., leading to self-realization. Both accept Veda authority.

6. Main idea of Samkhya?

Radical dualism: two eternal realities – Purusha (pure consciousness, witness) and Prakriti (primordial matter with three gunas: sattva, rajas, tamas). Suffering arises from confusing Purusha with Prakriti. Liberation (Kaivalya) occurs when one knows "I am Purusha, distinct from matter".

7. Astika vs Nastika difference?

Astika: Accept Veda as valid authority (Nyaya, Yoga, Vedanta). Believe in Atman, karma, rebirth. Nastika: Reject Vedic authority – Buddhism (anatman), Jainism (many souls), Charvaka (materialism). Charvaka denies afterlife; Buddhism rejects permanent self.

8. What is Pramana?

Pramana means valid means of acquiring true knowledge (Prama). Various schools accept different pramanas: perception (Pratyaksa), inference (Anumana), verbal testimony (Sabda), comparison (Upamana), presumption (Arthapatti), non-cognition (Anupalabdhi). Essential for epistemology.

9. Define Pratyaksa.

Perception – direct knowledge through sense-object contact. Most basic pramana. It requires functioning senses, proper conditions, and no distortion (e.g., jaundice makes white appear yellow). Divided into indeterminate (initial) and determinate (with recognition).

10. What is Anumana? Example.

Inference – knowledge derived from a sign that is invariably associated with the inferred object. Example: seeing smoke on a hill → infer fire, because smoke and fire have invariable concomitance (vyapti). Used widely in Nyaya and Buddhist logic.

11. Sabda Pramana?

Verbal testimony – knowledge from trustworthy sources (guru, scriptures, experts). In IKS, Vedas/Upanishads are primary Sabda for spiritual matters. Requires the speaker to be truthful, competent, and free from defects. Essential for understanding dharma and invisible realities.

12. Concept of Brahman?

Brahman is ultimate, eternal, formless reality – the cause and substratum of the universe. In Advaita Vedanta, Brahman is nirguna (without attributes), Sat-Chit-Ananda (Existence-Consciousness-Bliss). All creation arises from Brahman and dissolves into it. "Tat Tvam Asi" – thou art that.

13. What is Atman?

Atman is the innermost eternal self, pure consciousness, not body, mind, or ego. It remains unchanged through birth and death. In Vedanta, Atman is identical with Brahman. Realizing this identity through self-inquiry leads to Moksha. Buddhism denies a permanent Atman (Anatman).

14. Define Karma.

Karma means action and its inevitable moral consequence – every intentional action produces corresponding result (good → happiness, bad → suffering). It spans multiple lifetimes and determines rebirth conditions. Not fatalistic because present actions can modify past karma. Provides justice and moral responsibility.

15. What is Moksha?

Moksha is liberation from Samsara (cycle of birth, death, rebirth). It is the ultimate goal of Indian philosophy. In Advaita, it is realization that Atman = Brahman; in Buddhism, Nirvana (extinction of craving); in Jainism, Kevala (omniscience). Permanent cessation of suffering.

16. Two ancient texts and contributions?

Vedas: hymns, rituals, cosmology, phonetics, astronomy (Jyotisha), foundational philosophy. Arthashastra (Kautilya): statecraft, economics, espionage, diplomacy, public welfare (Yogakshema), military strategy – 4th century BCE.

17. Importance of Upanishads?

Upanishads (c.800-200 BCE) form the core of Vedanta philosophy. They shift focus from external Vedic rituals to inner self-inquiry. Explain Brahman, Atman, karma, moksha, and mahavakyas like "Tat Tvam Asi". Influenced all Indian philosophy, Buddhism, and Jainism.

18. Ethical value of Ahimsa?

Ahimsa = non-violence in thought, word, deed toward all living beings. Highest virtue in Jainism (even microorganisms) and central to Buddhism & Yoga. Gandhi used Ahimsa as core of India's freedom struggle, influencing global civil rights movements (Martin Luther King Jr.). Active compassion.

19. What is Samsara?

Samsara is the continuous cycle of birth, life, death, and rebirth (reincarnation) driven by karma and ignorance (Avidya). Characterized by suffering, impermanence, repeated death. Where one is reborn depends on accumulated karma. Moksha is release from Samsara.

20. Cyclical concept of time?

Time repeats in infinite cycles of creation, preservation, dissolution (Yugas: Satya, Treta, Dvapara, Kali). Each Yuga cycle = 4.32 million years. After Kali Yuga, universe resets. Contrasts with linear time (unique beginning and end). Encourages patience and ecological balance.

21. What is Rta?

Rta is cosmic order – governs natural regularity (seasons, planetary motion) as well as moral and ritual order. First mentioned in Rigveda. Dharma derives from Rta. Gods themselves uphold Rta. Similar to Greek Logos – principle of truth, justice, harmony.

22. Gurukul system briefly.

Residential education where students (brahmacharis) live with guru in natural setting. Holistic learning: Vedas, ethics, logic, martial arts, practical skills. Oral, personalized, experiential. Character formation, discipline, respect for guru. Duration 8–20 years. Gurudakshina offered at end.

23. Oral tradition.

Knowledge transmitted verbally without writing – precise memorization using mnemonic techniques (patha, krama, jata, ghana). Vedas preserved for 5,000+ years without alteration. Develops sharp memory, concentration, reverence for knowledge. Primary method even after writing emerged.

24. Two characteristics of ancient Indian education.

1) Holistic development: physical (yoga), mental (logic), moral (dharma), spiritual (meditation). 2) Oral tradition and memorization: knowledge transmitted through chanting, discussion, debate – not rote but lived.

25. Significance of Yoga in IKS?

Yoga is both a philosophical school (Astika) and a practical discipline. Ashtanga (8 limbs) systematically controls mind, integrates ethics (yama/niyama), body (asana), breath (pranayama), meditation (dhyana). Leads to self-realization and liberation – accessible to all, not just ascetics.

📖 Chapter 1 – Long Answers (8–10 marks)

1. Meaning, scope and significance of IKS

Meaning: IKS refers to systematic indigenous knowledge spanning philosophy, science, medicine, governance, arts, spirituality. Interdisciplinary, holistic.
Scope: 6 Astika + 3 Nastika schools, mathematics, Ayurveda, Arthashastra, Vastu, Jyotisha, Panini grammar, Gurukul education.
Significance: Provides solutions for modern crises – sustainable living, mental health (yoga), preventive medicine (Ayurveda), ethical governance (Arthashastra), environmental protection (Dharma). NEP 2020 integrates IKS for decolonization and holistic education.

2. Philosophical foundations & major schools

Epistemology: Pramanas (perception, inference, testimony). Metaphysics: Atman-Brahman, karma-samsara-moksha. Ethics: Dharma, Ahimsa.
Astika (accept Vedas): Nyaya (logic), Vaisheshika (atomism), Samkhya (dualism), Yoga (practice), Mimamsa (ritual), Vedanta (non-dualism).
Nastika (reject Vedas): Buddhism (anatman, 4NT), Jainism (Anekantavada, Ahimsa), Charvaka (materialism). All share debate tradition and soteriological concern.

3. Compare Astika and Nastika with examples

Veda authority: Astika accept Sabda; Nastika reject. Atman: Astika believe eternal self; Buddhism denies (anatman). Example: Vedanta (Astika) teaches Atman=Brahman; Charvaka (Nastika) denies afterlife, only perception valid. Jainism (Nastika) accepts many souls but rejects Veda. Despite differences, all engage in rigorous logic & ethics.

4. Concept of Pramanas (means of knowledge)

Pratyaksa (perception): direct sense contact – basic.
Anumana (inference): smoke→fire.
Sabda (testimony): scriptures/experts.
Upamana (comparison): unknown by analogy.
Arthapatti (presumption): implied fact.
Anupalabdhi (non-cognition): knowledge of absence. Charvaka accepts only perception; Nyaya accepts 4; Advaita accepts all 6. Pramanas ensure systematic validation of truth.

5. Metaphysical vision: Brahman, Atman, Karma, Moksha

Brahman: formless, eternal, Sat-Chit-Ananda, cause of universe.
Atman: individual self – identical to Brahman in Advaita.
Karma: moral law of cause-effect across lifetimes – determines rebirth.
Moksha: liberation from Samsara, end of suffering. Ignorance of Atman-Brahman identity causes karma & rebirth; knowledge leads to moksha.

6. Multidisciplinary contributions of ancient texts

Vedas: cosmology, rituals, phonetics. Upanishads: philosophy (Brahman, Atman). Epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata/Gita): ethics, dharma, psychology. Arthashastra: political science, economics. Charaka & Sushruta Samhitas: medicine, surgery. Panini's Ashtadhyayi: generative grammar. Natyashastra: performing arts. All demonstrate interdisciplinary integration.

7. Ethical and spiritual framework interconnection

Ethics (Yama: Ahimsa, Satya) purifies mind – prerequisite for meditation (spirituality). Karma directly links moral action to spiritual progress. Dharma is both social duty and cosmic law. Without ethical conduct, spiritual practices lead to ego, not liberation. Example: Buddha's Eightfold Path includes right action, right speech, right mindfulness – inseparable.

8. Cyclical time vs linear models

Indian (Cyclical): infinite cycles of creation/dissolution (Yugas: Satya→Kali). No single beginning, events repeat patterns. Encourages patience, ecological sustainability, long-term thinking.
Linear (Western/Abrahamic): unique beginning (Genesis/Big Bang) → unique end (eschaton/heat death). Promotes progress urgency but also existential anxiety. Indian cosmology accepts vast time scales (billions of years).

9. Gurukul system & knowledge transmission

Residential: students live with guru 8-20 years. Methods: oral tradition (shrutis, complex mnemonics), debate (shastra-arth), experiential learning (apprenticeship), self-study. Subjects: Vedas, logic, ayurveda, archery. Character building emphasized. Relevance today: mentorship, values, holistic learning (NEP 2020).

10. Holistic education & modern relevance

Ancient education developed physical (yoga), intellectual (logic), emotional (arts), social (dharma), spiritual (meditation). No separation of disciplines. Modern relevance: combats mental health crisis (mindfulness), hyper-specialization (interdisciplinary), value erosion (ethics). NEP 2020 integrates IKS to produce balanced, creative citizens.

11. Role of Anubhava, logic & tradition

Anubhava (experience): final validation of truth (e.g., self-realization). Logic (Nyaya): tests consistency, eliminates fallacies. Tradition (Shastra): provides foundational framework. All three work together – tradition gives initial direction, logic refines, experience confirms. Balanced epistemology avoids blind faith or rootless speculation.

12. How IKS integrates science, philosophy & spirituality

Example: Ayurveda – science (herbal pharmacology, anatomy) + philosophy (Tridosha theory, Samkhya metaphysics) + spirituality (mind-body connection, ethical living). Similarly, Jyotisha: astronomy (math/observation) + philosophy (karma) + ritual application. No rigid compartmentalization – knowledge is one unified whole aimed at human flourishing.

🔢 Chapter 2 – Short Answers (4 marks)

1. Holistic nature of IKS?
No isolation of disciplines – science, art, spirituality integrated. Example: Vastu includes math, astronomy, art, environment – all interconnected.
2. Decimal place-value system?
Digit value depends on position (units, tens) and zero as placeholder. Developed in India by 5th CE, transmitted to Islamic world & Europe – foundation of modern math.
3. Significance of zero (śūnya)?
Placeholder & number – Brahmagupta formalized rules: a+0=a, a×0=0. Enabled algebra, calculus, computing. Philosophically represents shunyata (emptiness).
4. Who was Aryabhata?
476 CE mathematician-astronomer, wrote Aryabhatiya. Contributions: decimal system, π=3.1416, sine/cosine, Earth rotation, scientific eclipses (not demons).
5. Two works of Bhaskara II?
Lilavati (arithmetic, geometry, algebra) and Siddhanta Shiromani (astronomy, planetary math, calculus ideas).
6. What is Jyotisha?
Indian astronomy & astrology – one of six Vedangas. Calculates planetary positions, eclipses, muhurta (auspicious times). Integrates math and spirituality.
7. What is Ayurveda?
"Science of Life", balances Vata, Pitta, Kapha (Tridosha). Emphasizes prevention (Dinacharya, Ritucharya), natural remedies, Panchakarma detoxification.
8. Two classical Ayurveda texts?
Charaka Samhita (internal medicine, Tridosha) and Sushruta Samhita (surgery, 300+ procedures, 120 instruments).
9. Father of surgery in India?
Sushruta (6th century BCE) – pioneer of rhinoplasty (nose reconstruction), cataract couching, lithotomy; described surgical instruments and training.
10. Tridosha theory?
Health = balance of Vata (air/space), Pitta (fire/water), Kapha (water/earth). Each person has unique Prakriti (constitution). Imbalance causes disease.
11. Define Ritucharya?
Seasonal regimen – adjusting diet, lifestyle, sleep according to six seasons (Vasanta, Grishma, Varsha, etc.) to maintain dosha balance and prevent disease.
12. Preventive healthcare in Ayurveda?
Swasthavritta – daily routines (Dinacharya), seasonal routines (Ritucharya), ethical conduct (Sadvritta), and Rasayana (rejuvenation). Proactive, reduces disease burden.
13. What is Arthashastra?
Kautilya's treatise on statecraft, economics, espionage, diplomacy. Covers taxation, law, military, and Yogakshema (public welfare). 4th century BCE.
14. Who was Kautilya (Chanakya)?
4th-century BCE strategist, economist, royal advisor to Chandragupta Maurya. Authored Arthashastra – pioneer of political science & realpolitik in India.
15. What is Yogakshema?
State's duty to ensure welfare (yoga) + security (kshema) of citizens – food, shelter, employment, justice, healthcare. Ancient Indian welfare state concept.
16. Stepwells used for?
Rainwater harvesting, year-round water supply, natural cooling, social/religious gatherings, groundwater recharge. Example: Rani ki Vav (UNESCO), Chand Baori.
17. Dharma in ecological context?
Moral duty to protect nature – sacred groves (Devarakadus), rivers as goddesses, Ahimsa toward animals, ban on cutting certain trees. Contrasts with exploitation.
18. What is Dana?
Selfless giving – food (Anna Dana), knowledge (Vidya Dana), protection (Abhaya Dana). Must be given without pride or expectation. Purifies mind, reduces inequality.
19. Gurukul relevance today?
Personalized mentorship, character development, experiential learning, holistic education. NEP 2020 integrates Gurukul elements: values, interdisciplinary, guru-shishya bond.
20. What is Nyaya logic?
5-step syllogism: proposition (pratijna), reason (hetu), example (udaharana), application (upanaya), conclusion (nigamana). Rigorous debate rules, fallacy detection.
21. Panini's contribution to linguistics?
Ashtadhyayi (4000 rules) – world's first generative grammar. Uses meta-language, algorithms. Influenced Saussure & Chomsky. Describes Sanskrit perfectly.
22. Interdisciplinary learning?
Integrates multiple subjects to solve complex problems. IKS natural example: studying a river = geography + history + biology + spirituality + economics + art.
23. What is Ahimsa?
Non-violence in thought, word, deed toward all beings. Highest virtue in Jainism, Buddhism, Yoga. Gandhi used Ahimsa for freedom struggle; active compassion.
24. Holistic vs reductionist?
Reductionist = break into isolated parts (modern Western science). Holistic = study whole system where parts interact (IKS). Both are complementary, not opposed.
25. Global recognition of IKS?
UNESCO Intangible Heritage: Yoga, Kumbh Mela. WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy includes Ayurveda. Zero/decimal taught globally. Mindfulness (Vipassana) in psychology.

🩺 Chapter 2 – Long Answers (8–10 marks)

1. Indian contributions to mathematics (zero, decimal, scholars)
Zero: Brahmagupta formalized rules (a+0=a, a×0=0). Decimal system: place-value (units, tens) developed by 5th century CE. Scholars: Aryabhata (π=3.1416, sine), Bhaskara II (Lilavati, calculus ideas). These innovations revolutionized world mathematics and enabled modern computing.
2. Scientific contributions of Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara II
Aryabhata: Earth rotates daily, scientific eclipses (shadows, not demons), solar year 365.3586 days. Brahmagupta: gravity as attractive force, zero rules, interpolation. Bhaskara II: instantaneous motion (differential calculus), planetary models, Rolle's theorem special case.
3. Ayurveda principles & modern relevance
Principles: Tridosha (Vata-Pitta-Kapha), Prakriti (individual constitution), Agni (digestive fire), Panchakarma (detox). Relevance: effective for lifestyle diseases (diabetes, hypertension), mental health (Shirodhara), immunity (Rasayana), WHO recognized, low side effects.
4. Sushruta surgical contributions & significance
Contributions: Rhinoplasty (forehead flap – still used), cataract couching, lithotomy (stone removal), 120 instruments, 8 surgical branches. Significance: First systematic surgical text, influenced Arabian & European surgery, ethical training (operate on gourds first).
5. IKS in governance – Arthashastra
Saptanga: 7 elements of state (king, ministers, territory, fort, treasury, army, allies). Mandala theory: circle of states (ally/enemy). Shadguna: 6 strategies (peace, war, neutrality, etc.). Yogakshema: public welfare. Relevance today: strategic realism, anti-corruption, disaster management.
6. Sustainability in Indian traditions
Examples: Sacred groves (Devarakadus) protect biodiversity; stepwells (baolis) harvest rainwater; Ahimsa promotes vegetarianism; Vrikshayurveda (tree medicine); cyclical time prevents overexploitation. These offer solutions for climate change & water security.
7. Integration of IKS in modern education & research
NEP 2020: mandatory IKS modules, IKS centers in universities. Research: clinical trials for Ayurveda, manuscript digitization (National Mission for Manuscripts), Yoga therapy for PTSD, computational Panini grammar. Interdisciplinary collaboration with traditional practitioners.
8. Ethical values: Dharma, Ahimsa, Dana in social welfare
Dharma: righteous duty – prevents exploitation, protects environment. Ahimsa: reduces violence, basis for conflict resolution (Gandhi). Dana: charity reduces inequality – midday meals (Anna Dana), free hospitals, temple annadanams. These build social capital.
9. Relevance of IKS for global challenges
Climate change: sacred groves, stepwells. Mental health: Yoga, meditation, Shirodhara. Antibiotic resistance: Ayurvedic antimicrobials (neem, turmeric). Inequality: Dana, Yogakshema. IKS offers complementary paradigms, but needs scientific validation.
10. Indian vs Western knowledge traditions
Indian: holistic, cyclical time, multiple pramanas (incl. testimony), inner transformation focus. Western: reductionist, linear time, empirical/positivist, objective observer. Both value logic & truth. Complementarity: Indian fills gaps in consciousness studies, holistic health; Western validates via RCTs.
11. Global recognition & reinterpretation of IKS
Recognition: UNESCO Yoga, UN Yoga Day, WHO Ayurveda, global zero/decimal, mindfulness in corporations. Reinterpretation risks: Yoga reduced to fitness, Ayurveda commercialized, loss of spiritual context. Need careful scholarship to preserve authenticity.
12. IKS for interdisciplinary & holistic development
Example: Vastu Shastra needs math + astronomy + art + environment + spirituality. Ayurveda needs biology + chemistry + psychology + ethics. IKS trains whole person – body (yoga), mind (logic), spirit (meditation). Essential for STEAM education & systems thinking.
